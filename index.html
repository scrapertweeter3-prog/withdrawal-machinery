The savings rate works twice

Two savers earn the same $100,000. One puts away 10 percent, spends the other $90,000, and needs a portfolio worth 25 times that spending, $2,250,000, before work turns optional. At a 5 percent real return, ten thousand a year reaches that target in about 51 years. The other puts away 40 percent, lives on $60,000, and needs $1,500,000; forty thousand a year fills the smaller target in about 22 years. Same income, same market, three decades apart, and neither one picked a better fund.

The gap is not one machine working harder, it is two machines pointed at the same finish line. Contributions are four times larger, which is the obvious half. The hidden half is the target: a portfolio sized at 25 times spending shrinks the moment spending shrinks, so the 40 percent saver aims at a line $750,000 closer. Walking the rate up in steps shows where the leverage lives. Ten to 25 percent cuts about 19 years; 25 to 40 cuts about 10 more; 40 to 50, five more. The first raise of the rate is the expensive one to skip.

This is the arithmetic under the advice to price a desired salary like an investor: the number that matters is not the income line, it is the fraction of income that never converts into lifestyle. A raise spent in full enlarges the target 25 times as fast as it enlarges the paycheck. A raise banked whole shortens the road from both ends at once, and no fund choice reproduces that.
